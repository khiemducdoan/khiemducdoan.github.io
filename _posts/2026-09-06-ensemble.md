# Ensembles in Statistical Physics: Microcanonical and Canonical

This note summarizes the basics of the microcanonical and canonical ensembles, derives key equations, and gives intuitive explanations and short proofs. The aim is clarity and usability for readers new to statistical physics.

## 1. What is an ensemble?

An ensemble is a large collection of virtual copies of a physical system, representing all possible microscopic states consistent with given macroscopic constraints. Ensembles let us compute average values of observables without tracking details of one system's dynamics.

Key idea: macroscopic properties = averages over microscopic states weighted by probabilities given by the ensemble.

## 2. Microcanonical ensemble

Applicable when the system is isolated: fixed energy E, volume V, and number of particles N.

- Microstates: all states with energy in a narrow interval [E, E + \delta E].
- Principle of equal a priori probabilities: each accessible microstate is equally likely.

Notation:
- \(\Omega(E)\): number (or density) of microstates at energy E.
- \(S\): entropy.

Fundamental relation (Boltzmann entropy):

\[ S(E,V,N) = k_B \ln \Omega(E,V,N). \]

Derivation (sketch):
- For isolated systems, assume equal probability p_i = 1/\Omega.
- Gibbs entropy reduces to Boltzmann form: \(S = -k_B \sum_i p_i \ln p_i = k_B \ln \Omega.\)

Thermodynamic quantities:
- Temperature: \(\frac{1}{T} = \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial E}\right)_{V,N}.\)
- Pressure: \(\frac{P}{T} = \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_{E,N}.\)

Intuition: entropy counts microstates; higher density of states -> higher entropy.

## 3. Canonical ensemble

Applicable when the system exchanges energy with a heat bath at fixed temperature T, but has fixed V and N.

Goal: find probability distribution over microstates of the system at temperature T.

Derivation of the canonical distribution (standard argument):

1) Consider system S weakly coupled to a large heat bath B with total energy E_total = E_S + E_B fixed.
2) Number of states for bath when system is in state i (energy E_i): \(\Omega_B(E_{\text{total}}-E_i)\).
3) Probability of state i is proportional to this number: \(p_i \propto \Omega_B(E_{\text{total}}-E_i)\).
4) For large bath, use \(\ln \Omega_B(E_{\text{total}}-E_i) \approx \ln \Omega_B(E_{\text{total}}) - \beta E_i\) where \(\beta = \frac{\partial \ln \Omega_B}{\partial E}|_{E_{\text{total}}} = 1/(k_B T)\).
5) Therefore \(p_i \propto e^{-\beta E_i}.\)

Normalizing gives the canonical (Gibbs) distribution:

\[ p_i = \frac{e^{-\beta E_i}}{Z}, \quad Z(\beta,V,N)=\sum_i e^{-\beta E_i}. \]

Partition function Z is central: it generates thermodynamic quantities.

Relations:
- Free energy: \(F = -k_B T \ln Z.\)
- Average energy: \(\langle E \rangle = -\frac{\partial}{\partial \beta} \ln Z.\)
- Entropy: \(S = -\sum_i p_i \ln p_i = k_B(\beta \langle E \rangle + \ln Z).\)
- Heat capacity: \(C_V = \frac{\partial \langle E \rangle}{\partial T} = k_B \beta^2 (\langle E^2 \rangle - \langle E \rangle^2).\)

Proof of average energy relation (brief):

\(\langle E \rangle = \sum_i p_i E_i = \frac{1}{Z} \sum_i E_i e^{-\beta E_i} = -\frac{1}{Z} \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta} \sum_i e^{-\beta E_i} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial \beta} \ln Z.\)

## 4. Connection between ensembles

In the thermodynamic limit (N -> infinity, fixed density), ensembles are equivalent for bulk thermodynamic quantities. Fluctuations relative to means go to zero for extensive variables.

Example (energy fluctuations in canonical ensemble):

Variance: \(\sigma_E^2 = \langle E^2 \rangle - \langle E \rangle^2 = k_B^2 T^2 C_V.\)

Relative fluctuation: \(\frac{\sigma_E}{\langle E \rangle} \propto 1/\sqrt{N} \to 0\) as N grows.

Thus canonical and microcanonical give same predictions for large systems.

## 5. Gibbs ensemble and generalized ensembles

- Grand canonical ensemble: exchange of particles and energy with a reservoir. Probabilities include chemical potential \(\mu\): \(p_i \propto e^{-\beta(E_i - \mu N_i)}\).
- Other ensembles impose different constraints; use maximum entropy principle to derive distributions with given constraints.

Maximum Entropy viewpoint (short):
Maximize Gibbs entropy S = -k_B sum_i p_i ln p_i subject to constraints (normalization and average energy, etc.). Use Lagrange multipliers to obtain canonical (or other) distributions.

## 6. Quick derivation via MaxEnt (canonical)

Maximize L = -k_B \sum_i p_i \ln p_i - \alpha (\sum_i p_i -1) - \beta (\sum_i p_i E_i - \langle E \rangle).
Setting \(\partial L/\partial p_i = 0\) gives \(-k_B(\ln p_i +1) - \alpha - \beta E_i =0\) -> \(p_i \propto e^{-\beta E_i}\).

Interpretation: \(\beta\) is the Lagrange multiplier associated with energy and equals 1/(k_B T).

## 7. Simple examples

- Two-level system (energies 0 and \(\epsilon\)): \(Z = 1 + e^{-\beta \epsilon}\), \(\langle E \rangle = \epsilon/(1 + e^{\beta \epsilon}).\)
- Harmonic oscillator (quantum): \(Z = 1/(2\sinh(\beta \hbar \omega/2)).\)

## 8. Helpful tips for understanding

- Think of entropy as logarithmic counting of states.
- Canonical distribution arises from weighing system states by how many bath states they allow.
- Partition function is the generating function for thermodynamics.
